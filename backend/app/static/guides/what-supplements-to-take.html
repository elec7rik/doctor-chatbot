What Supplements Should You Actually Take?

Walk into any pharmacy and the supplement wall is overwhelming — and most of it, for most people, is money you don't need to spend. But a small handful genuinely fill common gaps. This guide cuts through the noise: what's worth considering, what's overhyped, and how to buy well.

Food first — the honest bit

No pill out-performs a decent diet, and "supplement" means exactly that — something that fills a gap a varied diet leaves behind. If your plate is mostly whole foods, plenty of plants, enough protein and some oily fish, you already have most bases covered. Supplements are for plugging specific, likely gaps — not for buying your way out of a poor diet.

The short list worth considering

These have the best mix of evidence and real-world usefulness for a general adult:

  • Vitamin D Strong — the one nearly everyone in the UK should think about. Because of our latitude, we make little from sunlight in autumn and winter, and the NHS advises most people consider 10 micrograms (400 IU) a day over those months.
  • Omega-3 (EPA/DHA) Moderate — if you rarely eat oily fish, a fish-oil (or algae-based) supplement is a reasonable way to top up. Supports heart and general health.
  • Magnesium Promising — many people don't reach the recommended intake. Involved in sleep, muscle function and hundreds of reactions; gentle forms like glycinate are easy on the stomach.
  • Creatine monohydrate Strong — the most-studied sports supplement there is, safe and cheap, with growing evidence for cognition too. Not just for bodybuilders.
  • Protein powder Situational — not magic, just convenient protein if you struggle to hit your needs from food, which matters more as you age.
  • A basic multivitamin Modest — reasonable low-dose "insurance" if your diet is patchy, but it won't fix a poor diet and isn't essential for everyone.

Popular but overhyped or situational

  • Greens powders — convenient, but no substitute for actual vegetables and often over-marketed.
  • "Fat burners" and most testosterone boosters — long on claims, short on evidence.
  • Mega-dose antioxidants — more isn't better; very high doses can even work against you.
  • Collagen Promising — a genuine exception among the trendy ones, with some evidence for skin and joints; situational rather than essential.

How to choose a decent supplement

  • Sensible doses. Match the recommended intake; skip anything screaming "5000% of your RDA".
  • The right form. Some forms absorb better or sit easier (e.g. magnesium glycinate over oxide).
  • Third-party testing. Look for independent quality/purity testing where you can.
  • Fewer, better products. Three things you'll actually take beat a cupboard of twelve you won't.

Safety and interactions

"Natural" doesn't mean "harmless". Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) and minerals like iron can build up if over-done, and some supplements interact with medicines — vitamin K with blood thinners, St John's Wort with many drugs, and others besides. If you're pregnant, breastfeeding, on medication, or managing a health condition, run your list past a pharmacist or GP first. It's a two-minute conversation that can save you real trouble.

Frequently asked questions

What supplements should I actually take?

For most UK adults the short list worth considering is vitamin D (especially in autumn and winter), omega-3 if you rarely eat oily fish, magnesium, and creatine. A basic multivitamin can be modest insurance if your diet is patchy. Food comes first.

Do I really need a multivitamin?

Not necessarily. A low-dose multivitamin is reasonable insurance if your diet is inconsistent, but it won't fix a poor diet and isn't essential for everyone. Targeting specific likely gaps (like vitamin D) is usually smarter.

Is vitamin D worth taking in the UK?

Yes for most people — because of our latitude we make little vitamin D from sunlight in autumn and winter, and the NHS advises considering 10 micrograms (400 IU) a day over those months.

How do I know a supplement is good quality?

Look for sensible doses that match recommended intakes, well-absorbed forms, and independent third-party testing. Fewer, better products you'll actually take beat a cupboard full you won't.

Are supplements safe to take with medication?

Not always — some interact with medicines (for example vitamin K with blood thinners, or St John's Wort with many drugs), and fat-soluble vitamins and minerals can build up if over-done. If you're pregnant, breastfeeding, on medication or managing a condition, check with a pharmacist or GP first.
