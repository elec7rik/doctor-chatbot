Low Testosterone: Signs, Causes and What Actually Helps

Testosterone gets a lot of hype online, much of it selling something. Low testosterone is a real medical thing — but it's also over-diagnosed by marketing. Here's an honest look at the signs, what genuinely affects your levels, and what actually helps.

Signs of low testosterone

Symptoms can include low libido, erectile difficulties, persistent fatigue, low mood, reduced muscle or strength, and trouble concentrating. The catch: every one of these overlaps with poor sleep, stress, depression and other common issues — which is exactly why you can't diagnose it from a symptom list alone.

What actually lowers it

  • Age — testosterone declines gradually from your 30s–40s, which is normal.
  • Excess body fat — one of the biggest modifiable factors.
  • Poor sleep — testosterone is largely produced during sleep; short nights lower it measurably.
  • Heavy alcohol and chronic stress.
  • Certain medical conditions and medications — which is another reason to get properly checked.

Get tested properly

If you have persistent symptoms, the right move is a morning blood test (levels are highest early) plus a proper assessment — not a self-diagnosis. Diagnosis and any treatment are a decision for a GP or endocrinologist. Buying testosterone online is illegal, unregulated and genuinely risky; don't.

Lifestyle levers that genuinely help

For most men with borderline or lifestyle-driven low levels, these have the best evidence:

  • Strength training — resistance exercise supports healthy testosterone and muscle.
  • Losing excess body fat — often the single biggest lever.
  • Sleep — protect it; see our sleep guide.
  • Moderating alcohol and managing stress.

Nutrients — and the "booster" myth

Fixing genuine deficiencies is where supplements matter. Zinc contributes to the maintenance of normal testosterone levels, and vitamin D and magnesium are worth keeping topped up if you're low. Beyond correcting deficiencies, most heavily marketed "testosterone boosters" (tribulus and friends) Overhyped don't stand up in trials. See our supplements guide.

A word on TRT

Testosterone replacement therapy is genuinely effective for men with medically confirmed low testosterone, under proper supervision and monitoring. It is not an anti-ageing shortcut or a substitute for the lifestyle basics, and it carries risks that need a doctor's oversight — so it's a clinical decision, not an online purchase.

When to see a doctor

See a GP if symptoms are persistent and affecting your life, particularly low libido, erectile problems, or ongoing fatigue and low mood. A simple assessment sorts out whether testosterone is actually the issue — or whether something else (like sleep, mood or thyroid) is the real culprit.

Frequently asked questions

What are the signs of low testosterone?

Common signs include low libido, erectile difficulties, persistent fatigue, low mood, and reduced muscle or strength. Because these overlap with sleep problems, stress and depression, a blood test and proper assessment are needed to confirm it.

How can I increase testosterone naturally?

The best-evidenced levers are strength training, losing excess body fat, protecting your sleep, and moderating alcohol and stress. Correcting deficiencies in zinc, vitamin D and magnesium can help; most marketed 'boosters' don't.

Do testosterone booster supplements work?

Beyond correcting a genuine deficiency (zinc contributes to maintaining normal testosterone levels), most heavily marketed testosterone boosters like tribulus don't hold up in trials.

Should I consider TRT?

Testosterone replacement therapy is effective for men with medically confirmed low testosterone under proper supervision, but it isn't an anti-ageing shortcut and carries risks. It's a decision to make with a GP or endocrinologist after testing — never an online purchase.
