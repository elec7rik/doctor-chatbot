Hair Loss and Hair Growth: What Actually Works

Hair loss is extremely common and rarely talked about honestly. The good news is that a couple of treatments are genuinely proven, some nutrition tweaks help if you're deficient, and a lot of what's sold is wishful thinking. Here's the straight version.

Why hair thins

  • Pattern hair loss (androgenetic alopecia) — the most common cause in both men and women, largely genetic and driven by the hormone DHT. It's gradual: a receding hairline or thinning crown in men, widening parting in women.
  • Telogen effluvium — temporary shedding a few months after stress, illness, surgery, childbirth or rapid weight loss. It usually recovers.
  • Nutrient and health issues — low iron (ferritin), thyroid problems, and crash dieting can all thin hair.

What actually works

Two treatments have the strongest evidence, and both are worth a proper conversation with a pharmacist or GP:

  • Minoxidil Strong — a topical (and now sometimes oral) treatment available over the counter that can slow loss and regrow some hair. It needs consistent, ongoing use to keep the benefit.
  • Finasteride Strong — a prescription tablet for men that blocks DHT and is effective for pattern loss. Because it's a prescription-only medicine in the UK, it needs a doctor, who'll talk you through the benefits and possible side effects.

For women, minoxidil plus treating any underlying cause (iron, thyroid) is the usual evidence-based route — a GP can guide this.

Nutrition for your hair

The rule here is the same as everywhere: fix a genuine deficiency and it helps; top up what you already have and it usually won't. Worth checking: iron/ferritin (a common, correctable cause of shedding), vitamin D, and enough protein. Biotin is heavily marketed for hair, but only actually helps the rare people who are deficient. The peptide research angle

Peptides are an active research area for skin and hair. GHK-Cu (copper peptide) in particular has drawn scientific interest for follicle and scalp-related pathways.

Important: research-grade GHK-Cu is sold for laboratory research use only — it is not a hair treatment, not a cosmetic, and not for human use. The proven options above are where to start if you actually want to keep your hair.

What's overhyped

Most "hair growth" gummies, biotin megadoses (if you're not deficient), and thickening serums promise far more than the evidence supports. Save your money for what works.

When to see a doctor

See a GP for sudden, patchy or rapid hair loss, loss with other symptoms (fatigue, weight change), or if it's affecting your confidence — some causes are very treatable, and getting the diagnosis right matters.

Frequently asked questions

What actually stops hair loss?

The two treatments with the strongest evidence are minoxidil (topical, over the counter) and, for men, finasteride (a prescription tablet that blocks DHT). Both need ongoing use and are worth discussing with a pharmacist or GP.

Does biotin help hair growth?

Biotin only helps the small number of people who are genuinely deficient. For everyone else, the marketing far outstrips the evidence. Checking iron, thyroid and protein intake is usually more useful.

Can peptides regrow hair?

Copper peptides such as GHK-Cu are an area of research interest for hair and scalp, but research-grade GHK-Cu is sold for laboratory research use only — not as a hair treatment and not for human use. Proven options like minoxidil come first.

When should I see a doctor about hair loss?

See a GP for sudden, patchy or rapid loss, loss alongside other symptoms like fatigue or weight change, or if it's affecting your wellbeing. Several causes are very treatable once diagnosed.
