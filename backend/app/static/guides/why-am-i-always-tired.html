Why Am I Always Tired? How to Get Your Energy Back

By the My Longevity Hub editorial team · Updated September 2026 · 4 min read

Energy is what's left once your body has covered sleep, stress, food and movement. Here's how to find the leaks — and where nutrients, and NAD+, genuinely fit.

"Why am I so tired all the time?" is one of the most common questions we get — and it rarely has a single, exciting answer. Energy is what's left over once your body has covered sleep, stress, food, movement and any underlying issues. Fix the leaks and it usually returns.

One important note up front: persistent, unexplained tiredness deserves a proper medical look, because a few very treatable things (low iron, an underactive thyroid, low B12 or vitamin D) hide behind it. More on that below.

The common causes of low energy

  • Not enough good sleep — the obvious one, and the biggest. (Our sleep guide covers this.)
  • Blood-sugar swings from meals heavy in refined carbs and light on protein — the classic mid-afternoon crash.
  • Dehydration — even mild fluid loss shows up as fatigue and fuzzy thinking.
  • Doing too little — counter-intuitively, being sedentary breeds tiredness; regular movement raises energy.
  • Chronic stress — running on adrenaline all day is exhausting.
  • Nutrient gaps — low iron (especially in menstruating women), low B12 (common in vegans and older adults) and low vitamin D can all sap energy.
  • Medical causes — thyroid problems, anaemia, sleep apnoea, low mood, and some medications.

Rebuilding your energy (the foundations)

  • Protect your sleep first. Nothing on this list works if you're short on sleep.
  • Anchor meals with protein and fibre to flatten the blood-sugar rollercoaster and the crashes that come with it.
  • Move most days. Even a brisk daily walk reliably lifts energy and mood over a few weeks.
  • Hydrate — keep water within reach; thirst is a late signal.
  • Get morning light and take real breaks to keep stress from grinding you down.
  • Use caffeine as a tool, not a crutch — helpful in the morning, counter-productive if it's papering over poor sleep or creeping into the afternoon.

Nutrients and supplements linked to energy

The single most useful principle here: correct a genuine deficiency and you'll feel it; top up something you already have enough of and you usually won't. Where supplements can matter:

  • Iron Strong (if deficient) — iron-deficiency anaemia is a real, common cause of fatigue. But don't supplement blind: too much iron is harmful. Ask for a blood test first.
  • Vitamin B12 Strong (if deficient) — important for energy metabolism; worth checking if you're vegan, older, or on certain medications.
  • Vitamin D Moderate — low levels are widespread in the UK winter and linked to tiredness and low mood. The NHS suggests most people consider 10 micrograms daily in autumn and winter.
  • Magnesium Promising — involved in hundreds of energy-related reactions; many people fall short of the recommended intake.
  • Creatine Promising — best known for the gym, but there's growing evidence it also supports mental energy and may blunt the effects of sleep deprivation.
  • CoQ10 Mixed — popular for energy; the evidence in otherwise healthy people is inconsistent.

The NAD+ and ageing angle

If you're interested in the longevity side of energy, this is where it gets interesting. NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) is a coenzyme your cells use to turn food into usable energy inside the mitochondria. NAD+ levels fall as we age, and that decline is one of the threads researchers are pulling on to explain age-related dips in energy and resilience.

Supplementing with NAD+ precursors (or NAD+ directly) has been shown to raise NAD+ levels in humans, and the research into what that means for energy, metabolism and healthy ageing is active and promising — though long-term clinical outcomes are still being established. It's a genuinely exciting, fast-moving area rather than a settled cure, and worth understanding rather than over-claiming.

Red flags: when to see a doctor

Book an appointment if fatigue is severe, persistent (weeks), or unexplained, or if it comes with unintended weight loss, breathlessness, a racing or irregular heartbeat, or low mood. A simple set of blood tests (full blood count, ferritin, B12, vitamin D, thyroid function) sorts out most of the common, treatable causes — so it's a smart first step, not an over-reaction.

Frequently asked questions

Why am I so tired all the time?

The most common causes are not enough good sleep, blood-sugar swings from unbalanced meals, dehydration, being sedentary, chronic stress, and nutrient gaps like low iron, B12 or vitamin D. Persistent unexplained tiredness should be checked by a GP.

What vitamins help with tiredness?

Correcting a genuine deficiency helps most: iron (if you're low), vitamin B12, and vitamin D are the big three. Magnesium and creatine may help some people. Topping up nutrients you already have enough of usually won't move the needle.

Does NAD+ boost energy?

NAD+ is a coenzyme central to how cells produce energy, and its levels fall with age. Supplements can raise NAD+ levels in humans, and the research on energy and healthy ageing is promising, though long-term outcomes are still being established.

When should I see a doctor about fatigue?

See a GP if tiredness is severe, lasts for weeks, or is unexplained — especially with weight loss, breathlessness, palpitations or low mood. A simple blood panel (iron, B12, vitamin D, thyroid, full blood count) finds most treatable causes.
